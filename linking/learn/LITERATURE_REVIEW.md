# Feed the swarm thousands of shapes first

The research literature backs your plan's ordering at the top and is much less sure at the bottom. **Step 1, the random shape generator, has the strongest evidence of the four**: in the closest learned-assembly study, a 16-block structure trained on by itself was built **0% of the time, against 90.9% when it was trained alongside many other blueprints** ([Blocks Assemble, ICML 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.13733v2)), and game-level research found that **up to about 10,000 generated levels** were needed before test performance caught up with training performance ([Procgen, ICML 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/cobbe20a/cobbe20a.pdf)). **Step 2 is the mainstream recipe**, keeping a teacher that sees more than the student and correcting the student with DAgger, but 2024–2025 papers add two changes: make the teacher "student-aware", and score agreement against the teacher's *set* of equally good moves, not one random pick. **Step 3 is conditional.** Memory and learned messages help only when the bot truly lacks information, and learned messages are often quietly ignored. Keep your hand-built hop-count signal and treat learned messages as an add-on you must prove is used. **Step 4 is half settled.** Hard safety masks are well supported, and the papers explain how to fine-tune with RL without wrecking what imitation taught. No paper found shows an agent *discovering* temporary support pillars from reward alone, so that part is a real hope, not an established result. Above all, nobody appears to have published a learned, 3D, connectivity-constrained lattice swarm tested on held-out shape families. Your project sits in a genuine open niche, which also means much of the advice below is inference from neighbouring fields.

## How to read the evidence labels in this report

Each step below is split into **what the papers found** (cited), **what we infer for the microbots** (reasoning by the research team or this report, not something a paper tested), a **verdict with suggested changes**, and **what is genuinely unknown**. The source notes came from four research passes of roughly 20 searches and page fetches each, run in October 2026, and they carry caveats you should keep in view. **[preprint]** marks an arXiv paper with no confirmed peer-reviewed venue. **[snippet only]** means the researcher saw only a search-result excerpt, not the paper. **[not re-fetched]** marks an older, well-known paper cited from prior knowledge whose details were not re-checked this session. **[secondary]** means the claim came from a summary site, not the paper itself. Many 2026 items are very recent preprints, and one key swarm paper was posted this month. A table near the end lists the citations that look most doubtful.

A few terms recur. A **policy** is the rule a bot uses to pick its next move; yours is a small **MLP** (multi-layer perceptron, a basic neural network) shared by every bot. **Held-out** means kept out of training and used only for testing. **Out-of-distribution (OOD)** means unlike anything seen in training.

## Step 1: The shape generator is the best-supported lever

### What the papers found

The most useful single study is Blocks Assemble, where one robot policy assembled magnetic blocks into target blueprints. Its blueprints were **hand-designed, not generated**: 165 in total, split 141 for training and 24 for testing ([Blocks Assemble, arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.13733v2)). Three results matter for you. First, **variety itself was the cure**: the 0% versus 90.9% gap above came only from training on many blueprints together, and agents trained on one blueprint "mostly failed to transfer". Second, **the test split exposed a family gap**. Held-out large blueprints scored about as well as training ones (**89.6%**), but held-out "special" structures (the tallest, longest and letter-shaped ones, the closest thing to a held-out *family*) scored **77.5%**, roughly 10–12 points lower. Third, **network structure decided generalisation**: a policy that attends to blocks as separate objects reached 89.6%, removing that attention gave 3.6%, and a standard image network (ResNet) scored **0%** on held-out shapes ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/ghasemipour22a/ghasemipour22a.pdf)).

Research on **procedural content generation** (letting a program generate training levels instead of designing them by hand) gives the scale. Procgen trained game-playing agents on pools of 100 to 100,000 levels and saw heavy overfitting with small pools. Closing the gap took as many as 10,000 levels, and **training performance itself improved as the pool grew** ([Procgen](https://cdn.openai.com/procgen.pdf)). A navigation study found the same in miniature: 200 training houses generalised better than 20 ([House3D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.02209)). In 2024–2025, Kinetix trained one agent on **millions** of randomly generated 2D physics scenes and found it solved unseen hand-designed levels zero-shot (without extra training), though performance dropped on harder ones ([Kinetix, ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.23208v2); [GitHub](https://github.com/FlairOx/Kinetix)).

On *how* to pick training tasks, the field of **unsupervised environment design (UED)**, methods where a "teacher" program chooses which levels to train on, has a sobering 2024 result. In a robotics-inspired domain, **no state-of-the-art UED method beat plain domain randomisation** (just sampling levels uniformly at random). The method that did help, **Sampling For Learnability (SFL)**, trains most on levels the agent solves sometimes but not always ([No Regrets, NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/1d0ed12c3fda52f2c241a0cebcf739a6-Abstract.html)). An older method, ACCEL, makes new levels by small edits to ones the agent finds hard ([ACCEL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.01302)). Blocks Assemble's own "favour the shapes we fail" weighting had "not a clear-cut benefit".

Recent assembly work agrees that generalisation is mostly interpolation. A 2026 goal-conditioned builder "generalizes reasonably well" to small shifts but "is less reliable for unseen layouts that differ more substantially", at roughly **80% under shifted targets and 50% on in-between layouts** ([Learning to Build, arXiv 2602.23934](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23934)) [preprint]. A 2024 multi-agent Minecraft building benchmark reports that models "struggle" with new goals, scenes and team sizes ([TeamCraft](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05255.pdf)). Neural cellular automata (NCA), grids of cells that all run one small learned update rule, can grow many shapes when told which one to grow, but they are almost never tested on shapes they were not trained on ([Conditional Morphogenesis, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08360)) [preprint].

Theory says which shapes can be built at all, and the answer depends on how your bots move. For **sliding cubes** that stay face-connected, any connected shape can become any other in **O(n²) moves** (moves growing at most with the square of the bot count), and this bound is tight ([Abel et al., SoCG 2024](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2024.1)). For **pivoting cubes**, which swing over an edge and need empty room to do so, universality fails: in 2D it holds only with **5 extra helper modules** ([O(1) Musketeers](https://arxiv.org/html/1908.07880)), and in a related 3D pivoting model reconfiguration is **PSPACE-hard** (computationally intractable in general), with some configurations permanently **rigid** ([arXiv 2304.09990](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.09990)). Deciding whether a single parallel step suffices is NP-complete even for sliding squares ([Sliding Squares in Parallel](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05523)).

No paper compared ways of telling a builder *what* to build (a local slice of the target, one global code for the whole shape, or a distance field). The indirect evidence favours local and relational inputs, and categorical shape codes cannot by construction describe a new shape ([arXiv 2512.08360](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08360)). Swarm policies built on graph neural networks (GNNs, networks that pass information along a graph of neighbours) also degrade when test swarms are much denser or larger than training swarms ([arXiv 2409.19829](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.19829); [arXiv 2605.19209](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.19209)) [snippet only].

### What we infer for the microbots

*Inference:* two training shapes and one test shape puts the project in Blocks Assemble's single-blueprint regime, where transfer usually fails. Building the hollow tower was probably helped by the strong local design (5×5×5 view, shared MLP, hop count, compass), but one test shape cannot show generalisation. The working definition of "buildable" should follow the move model. If bots can always slide, "face-connected and touching the floor" is a safe class to sample from. If any moves are pivots, define **buildable as "the hand-written teacher can finish it"** and filter every generated shape through the teacher, or failures on hard families will mix up policy mistakes with impossible targets. The held-out families should isolate specific mechanisms: enclosed cavities (bots must not seal themselves in), overhangs and arches (need temporary support), one-voxel-wide slots (no room to pivot), and sizes beyond the training range. The current input design, a local target map plus a relayed field, matches the family the evidence favours. A "distance to nearest unfilled target voxel" channel is a natural next field to test, but no paper has tested it.

### Verdict and changes

**Supported, strongly.** Build the generator, aim for **thousands to tens of thousands** of teacher-solvable shapes, and start with uniform random sampling, the baseline most curricula fail to beat. Add an SFL-style rule later: oversample shapes where the swarm succeeds sometimes, and for DAgger, shapes where the student most often departs from the teacher. Use ACCEL-style "add or remove a few voxels" edits to make new hard shapes. Report **within-family held-out and cross-family held-out success separately**, since Blocks Assemble saw a 10+ point gap between them. Report move counts relative to n² as an efficiency yardstick from theory. Vary swarm size during training too.

### Genuinely unknown

No paper found trains a learned policy for connectivity-constrained lattice self-reconfiguration on generated shapes and reports held-out family results. Nobody has measured "how many shapes" for construction specifically: Procgen's numbers are for video-game levels. The best way to encode the target (local window, global code or distance field) has never been compared head-to-head. Each of these is a publishable gap.

## Step 2: Keep the teacher and DAgger, but grade the student fairly

### What the papers found

Training a **privileged teacher** (one that sees information the deployed robot will not have) and then copying it into a limited student is the default recipe in legged robots and driving. It started with "Learning by Cheating" in driving ([arXiv 1912.12294](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12294)) [not re-fetched] and became standard in quadruped locomotion ([Lee et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11251); [RMA, 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034)) [not re-fetched], with 2024 work still using the same two phases ([PA-LOCO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.04224)). The 2024–2025 consensus states when it breaks: when the student cannot work out the hidden information from what it sees, it gets **conflicting labels for identical observations** and drifts ([Kim et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09546)) [preprint]. This failure is called the **imitation gap**: plain copying recovers only the teacher's action *averaged over* what the student cannot see ([ADVISOR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12173)) [not re-fetched; a search snippet dated it 2024, but it is NeurIPS 2021 work].

Fixes fall into five families. The first mixes in reward, as in ADVISOR and TGRL ([TGRL, ICML 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03186)) [not re-fetched]. The second makes the teacher imitable: **Student-Informed Teacher Training** penalises the teacher for actions the student cannot reproduce ([Messikommer et al., ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09149)), and Guided Policy Optimization keeps a co-trained teacher inside the learner's reach ([GPO, arXiv 2505.15418](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.15418)) [preprint]. The third gives privileged information only to the critic, the part of an RL learner that judges how good a situation is ([Informed Asymmetric Actor-Critic, ICML 2026](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/ebi26a.html)). The fourth gives the student memory, discussed under Step 3. The fifth decides when to ask the teacher ([Kim et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09546)).

**DAgger** (Dataset Aggregation: the student drives, the teacher labels the states the student actually visits, and those labels are added to the training set) remains the standard fix for small errors snowballing ([Ross et al. 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)) [not re-fetched]. Recent variants mostly decide *when* to query the teacher. They carry a warning for teachers that have several equally good moves: methods that query the teacher whenever an ensemble of students disagrees "can mistake disagreement between policies for uncertainty at multimodal decision points" ([Diff-DAgger, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14868)) [preprint]. No paper found studies DAgger with a randomised *rule-based* teacher like yours.

On symmetry, the choice is **augmentation** (training on rotated and mirrored copies of each example) or **equivariance** (building the network so that rotating its input rotates its output automatically). A 2024 study found equivariant models more data-efficient, but augmented models **closed the gap given enough training** ([Brehmer et al., arXiv 2410.23179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.23179)) [workshop paper], and theory shows augmented networks converge toward equivariant behaviour in the very-wide-network limit ([arXiv 2406.06504](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.06504)). Forcing exact symmetry hurts when the world is only approximately symmetric ([Approximate Equivariance in RL, 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04225); [RLJ 2025](https://rlj.cs.umass.edu/2025/papers/RLJ_RLC_2025_310.pdf)). The closest robotics result: RL controllers for 2D pivoting-cube swarms that built in grid symmetries gained **"only minor training benefits"**, though the models could be smaller ([Dobreva et al., arXiv 2509.03140](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2509.03140)).

### What we infer for the microbots

*Inference:* the arch failure under a weaker local signal (2 of 5) is a textbook imitation gap. The teacher's choice depended on the global crawl map, which a 5×5×5 window cannot reveal, and the relayed hop count made that information inferable again. The **65% agreement** figure is likely misleading. A teacher that breaks ties randomly caps exact-match agreement far below 100%, so 65% agreement alongside full task success suggests most "disagreements" are between equally good moves. Better measures are whether the student's move is in the teacher's set of acceptable moves, the gap between the student's and teacher's move probabilities (KL divergence or cross-entropy), task success, and safety-mask hits. For training, **soft labels** (the teacher's full move distribution, or a uniform spread over its tied-best moves) remove label noise at no cost, and a standard softmax output already handles several valid discrete moves.

For symmetry, gravity fixes "up", so the valid group is the **8 symmetries that keep the vertical axis** (4 rotations about vertical times a mirror), not the full 48 symmetries of a cube. Every input (window, target map, hop count, compass) and every action label must be transformed together. Mirroring reverses handedness, so check the compass and any left/right action labels carefully. Because your simulator is cheap and the teacher labels unlimited data, augmentation should recover most of what an equivariant network would give, and it also averages out any directional bias in the teacher's tie-breaking. A no-retraining option is **symmetrisation**: run the MLP on all 8 transformed views, transform the outputs back, and average them. This is standard practice, but it was not separately sourced here.

### Verdict and changes

**Supported, with modifications.** Keep the global teacher and DAgger. Add the 8-element vertical-preserving augmentation, not the full 48-element group, and skip an equivariant architecture for now. Switch to soft labels and tie-aware metrics. Run more than two DAgger rounds, focused on states from the new generated shapes. If imitation-gap failures come back on harder families, make the teacher student-aware, for example a rule teacher limited to information the hop-count relay can carry. Avoid ensemble-disagreement gating, because your teacher is genuinely multimodal.

### Genuinely unknown

No published teacher-to-student distillation exists for modular or lattice robots, no study covers DAgger with a stochastic rule teacher, and no lattice-robot policy using cube-group equivariance turned up. Whether augmentation or symmetrisation matters more for held-out *families* (not just rotated copies of training shapes) is untested.

## Step 3: Memory and messages help only when bots truly lack information

### What the papers found

For **memory**, meaning internal state a bot carries from tick to tick, the RL benchmark POPGym found **GRUs (gated recurrent units, a small standard memory cell) the best general-purpose choice**, ahead of newer transformer and state-space designs, some of which were unstable ([POPGym, ICLR 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.01859)). An ICLR 2024 paper confirms GRU as the best model-free baseline there ([R2I](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/0a0e436e8830f746300f592377730fca-Paper-Conference.pdf)) [snippet only]. Memory is not free: on a 2025 pixel benchmark a plain MLP scored **0.45 against 0.42** for a minimal GRU overall ([POPGym Arcade](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2503.01450)) [snippet only]. The most directly relevant result comes from imitation learning with hidden context: **a history-aware student trained with on-policy data (DAgger) can recover the expert's performance where plain copying of histories fails** ([Swamy et al., NeurIPS 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02225)) [not re-fetched].

For **learned messages**, the evidence splits. GNN-style policies that pass messages between neighbours scale well to larger teams, in tasks such as navigation, coverage and flocking: from 3 to more than 100 robots ([Graph Policy Gradients](https://arxiv.org/html/1907.03822v4)) and from 100 to 500 ([arXiv 2409.19829](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.19829)) [snippet only]. Shared per-module policies passing messages along a robot's body generalise to unseen body layouts ([Pathak et al., NeurIPS 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.05546); [Huang et al., ICML 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.04976)) [not re-fetched]. In 2D pivoting-cube swarms, **nearest-neighbour information passed several times gave near-optimal reconfiguration** ([Dobreva et al.](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2509.03140)), the closest support for "a few rounds per tick".

The critiques of **emergent communication** (agents inventing their own message code from reward alone) are consistent, though. A 2025 workshop study **zeroed out learned 1-bit messages after training and success barely moved** (89.4% to 88.6%), and performance collapsed as the grid grew, while structured "intention" messages held near 97–100% ([Hill et al., arXiv 2508.02912](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.02912v2)) [workshop; no seeds or confidence intervals reported]. Its authors judge emergent communication "powerful but fragile". Older work warns that reward gains alone do not prove agents use messages ([Lowe et al., AAMAS 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.05168)) [not re-fetched], and surveys note benchmarks and bandwidth accounting are not standardised ([arXiv 2601.12886](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2601.12886)) [snippet only].

The NCA literature, structurally the closest to your planned student, adds two lessons. A 2026 survey names a **"global communication bottleneck"**, since information spreads only one cell per step, and notes most NCAs must be retrained per target ([Lemke et al., arXiv 2609.24595](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.24595v1)) [preprint]. A swarm-assembly NCA posted this month found that training on idealised whole-grid growth failed when real robots arrived one at a time: **F1 0.58** (a 0–1 shape-accuracy score), rising to **0.99** only after fine-tuning that mimicked gradual attachment and penalised false growth ([Verma et al., arXiv 2610.31900](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31900v2)) [preprint, 2D, one model per shape]. Training NCAs requires backpropagation through many steps (BPTT, training through a long unrolled sequence), which is slow and memory-hungry. It also needs stabilisers such as sample pools and gradient normalisation, and shows sudden loss spikes ([Growing NCA, Distill 2020](https://distill.pub/2020/growing-ca/)).

No paper compared a hand-designed gradient like a hop count against learned messages on the same assembly task. The classical systems that guarantee shape and build order, Kilobot ([Science 2014](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1254295)) and TERMES ([Science 2014](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1245842)), use hand-designed gradients and rules [not re-fetched].

### What we infer for the microbots

*Inference:* your relayed hop count already is a Kilobot-style gradient, and it fixed the arch. The evidence favours keeping it and adding a learned message channel *on top*, the hybrid that Verma and Hill both point toward. Imitation gives an advantage here that RL-only communication lacks. If messages are continuous and gradients flow through them within a tick (the DIAL style), every teacher label trains the channel directly, which is far easier than inventing symbols from sparse reward. Giving messages an **auxiliary target**, such as predicting a global quantity the simulator knows (distance to the nearest unsupported overhang, or "this region is about to become a sealed cavity"), should make them carry useful content. Memory plus messages turns the swarm into a spatially spread-out recurrent network. That eases the bottleneck but brings NCA-style training instability. Train inside the real slide/pivot simulator with incremental occupancy, not an idealised grid.

### Verdict and changes

**Conditionally supported. Do it after Step 1, and only where diagnostics show a need.** Use the generated hard families to find failures the 5×5×5 view plus hop count cannot resolve, then add a **small GRU** first, the best-evidenced and cheapest option, and learned messages second. Run three ablations every time: retrain with messages zeroed, zero them at test time, and probe whether messages predict non-local facts. Without these, any gain could just come from extra parameters. Train the recurrent student with DAgger, not plain copying.

### Genuinely unknown

No study isolates the benefit of memory for swarm or modular-robot agents. No 3D, connectivity-constrained, NCA- or GNN-controlled lattice assembly exists. The hop-count versus learned-message comparison, on held-out shapes with equal tuning, would be a novel result.

## Step 4: Masked RL is sound; learning to build supports is unproven

### What the papers found

Blocking unsafe moves before the policy chooses (an **action mask**, which formal-methods researchers call a *preemptive shield*) is well supported. Setting blocked moves' scores to minus infinity is still a valid way to train with policy gradients, and it matters more as the number of invalid moves grows ([Huang & Ontañón, FLAIRS 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14171)). A 2026 analysis shows that *not* masking causes "valid action suppression": training pushes down rarely legal moves even in states never visited ([Zabounidis et al., arXiv 2603.09090](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09090v1)) [preprint; 4 seeds, some differences within the spread]. The same paper found a pitfall: **agents trained only with a perfect mask collapsed (return about −0.9) when the mask changed**, while agents with an extra "is this move valid?" prediction head kept most of their performance. Construction work uses the same tool. A data-free physical-stability mask let RL reach **100% success on more than 250 Lego structures** ([Liu et al., arXiv 2408.10162](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10162)) [preprint], and the 2026 Learning to Build agent filters out unstable placements with a rigid-block equilibrium check before choosing ([arXiv 2602.23934](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23934)) [preprint].

That 2026 study also shows the limit of a yes/no check. On a real robot arm, **marginally stable structures collapsed**, because a binary stability test "cannot capture marginal stability"; the agent succeeded on 12 of 14 attempted tasks. It found emergent counterweights and arch-like solutions but **did not place temporary supports**. The nearest thing to learned supports is a 2025 study in which **robots themselves act as temporary supports**, holding parts instead of scaffolding while others place. It combined RL, a GPU stability simulator, a curriculum for sparse rewards, a GNN, and a trick of planning the *disassembly* and reversing it, on a 62-part vault ([Wang et al., ACM TOG 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3730824)) [experimental numbers not verified; PDF too large to fetch].

On **RL fine-tuning of an imitation-trained policy**, the 2024 consensus is that it works but **erodes skills in situations the fine-tuning does not revisit early**, often "catastrophically", and that standard retention tools fix most of it ([Wołczyk et al., ICML 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02868)). Two such tools are an extra loss for still matching the teacher's data and a **KL penalty** (a cost for drifting away from the original policy). Robotics papers from 2025 report the same: removing the KL penalty caused "substantial degradation" of a pretrained excavator skill ([ExT, arXiv 2509.14992](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14992)) [preprint]. **Residual RL**, which freezes the imitation policy and learns small corrections on top, raised real-robot success from about 14–23% to about 64% ([ResFiT, arXiv 2509.19301](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19301v2)) [preprint]. For many agents sharing one network, **MAPPO** (PPO, a standard RL algorithm, with shared weights and a shared judge of value) is a strong default. Sharing weights lifted hard StarCraft-map win rates from about 13% to about 90% ([Yu et al., NeurIPS 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.01955)). Kim et al. suggest starting RL episodes from known trouble states ([arXiv 2505.09546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09546)) [preprint].

On connector strength, simulators for learned construction usually reduce physics to one yes/no equilibrium check and ignore placement noise, connector give, marginal stability and forces during motion ([arXiv 2602.23934](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.23934)). The working hardware system, NASA's ARMADAS, avoids the problem with **strong reversible fasteners** and uses the lattice itself as a ruler, not learning ([Science Robotics 2024, NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230005194)). The research found **no 2024+ paper that models weak connector strength inside an RL training loop** for lattice robots. A 2026 opinion piece says most collective construction still uses hand-made rules and RL "is costly to train and sensitive to the environment" ([Construction Robotics 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41693-026-00216-x)).

### What we infer for the microbots

*Inference:* keeping the holder-tree mask is right, and replacing it with a "don't fall" penalty would be worse. "Learning can never drop a bot" holds only for failures the mask checks, though. If you add real ~3 N connector limits, put a **local load check into the mask itself**, as the Lego work did with its stability rule, and add a **graded margin** penalty (connector load as a fraction of capacity) on top, so the policy learns to stay away from the edge. If that loosens the mask relative to the holder tree, the policy faces a different set of legal moves than in imitation, which is exactly the mask-change collapse. An auxiliary "move valid?" head is the published hedge. Train the imitation student on masked outputs too, so it never puts probability on illegal moves; no paper tests this, so it is reasoning, not a result.

For the jammed arch, first check *why* it jams. Pivoting theory allows genuinely rigid or unreachable configurations, so the jam may be a move-model impossibility, not a planning mistake. If bots can slide, the universal O(n²) sliding algorithm could serve as a classical "un-jam" teacher, though it ignores gravity and strength. In a cube swarm the "scaffold" is other bots, so learning support pillars amounts to Wang et al.'s problem of deciding which agents hold and when to let go. Their curriculum and reverse-disassembly tricks are the most reusable ideas. Use a BC loss or KL penalty to the frozen DAgger policy, a regression suite of already-solved shapes, a dense "correct voxels filled" reward plus a completion bonus, a curriculum over span length, and a critic that sees the global crawl map while the bots' policy stays local.

### Verdict and changes

**The mask and the fine-tuning method are supported. The headline hope is unproven.** Keep the mask, make it strength-aware, add the validity head, protect imitation skills with KL or BC losses, and use MAPPO-style sharing with a privileged critic. Start episodes from jammed and bridge states. Treat "discovers support pillars without a planner" as the research question, not an expected outcome. Credit assignment (working out which few bots' choices earned a reward) will be hard when only a few bots act as supports.

### Genuinely unknown

Nobody has shown an agent discovering temporary, later-removed supports from reward alone. Nobody has done imitation-then-RL for lattice robots or construction swarms, reported how a KL penalty interacts with a hard mask, or quantified dynamic loads during pivots on weak connectors. Your Step 4 would be first on several counts, which is both the opportunity and the risk.

## Revised plan ordered by evidence

| Order | What to do | Strength of evidence | Why it sits here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Fix measurement: soft labels, tie-aware agreement, per-family success, teacher-solvability filter | Strong reasoning, low cost (inference) | Without it you cannot tell real gains from noise |

| 2 | 8-symmetry augmentation, more DAgger rounds on generated shapes, student-aware teacher if gaps recur | Strong for teacher plus DAgger; moderate for augmentation over equivariance | Cheap; mainstream recipe |
| 3 | Strength-aware mask with graded margin and validity head | Moderate (masking theory, Lego masks, EPFL failure analysis) | Needed before RL and for honest evaluation |

## Conclusion

The literature changes one assumption in the plan: the student's architecture is probably not your bottleneck yet. Blocks Assemble, Procgen and Kinetix agree that variety of training tasks moves generalisation more than model upgrades, and the 2D pivoting-cube RL work found symmetry-aware networks bought little. Memory and messages earn their place only once generated shapes reveal failures that local information cannot fix, so Step 1 doubles as the diagnostic that tells you whether Step 3 is needed. It is also worth checking whether your "65% agreement" problem is a scoring artefact before treating it as a learning problem.

The novel part of the project is the combination: 3D, gravity, a hard connectivity mask, weak connectors and learned local control. Every adjacent field has solved one or two of these, none all of them. That makes cheap, well-measured contributions possible early. A held-out-family benchmark for lattice assembly, a target-encoding comparison, and a hop-count versus learned-message ablation would each be new. The ambitious contribution, a swarm that learns to park bots as temporary pillars, has no precedent, so frame it as an experiment whose negative result would also be informative.
