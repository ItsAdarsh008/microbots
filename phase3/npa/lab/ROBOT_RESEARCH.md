## Section 1. One-page summary

### The field in plain words

**What modular-robot builders learned.** Robots that dock into structures (M-Blocks, SMORES, ATRON, Roombots, ModQuad, Swarm-bots) all hit the same three walls, in this order of pain:

1. **The connector is the weak point.** ATRON's authors list "connectors are often a weak point" and "stiffness of the structure when many modules are connected in a chain" as the standing problems of the whole field [S5, read]. M-Blocks say their magnet-bonded lattices are limited because bond strength is finite, and that bonds must survive the *impulses* during reconfiguration, which "may often exceed the static bonding forces" [S1, read]. SMORES' EP-Face connector holds 85-88 N pulling straight off, but only about 35-41 N sideways (shear), and its holding moment drops to about 10% of its straight-on value when the faces are twisted 25 degrees [S4, read]. Magnets are strong when pulled apart and comparatively weak when slid or twisted.
2. **Docking needs closed-loop alignment, and magnets forgive only a little.** SMORES splits docking into navigate, adjust pose (sideways offset and heading), then approach; magnets then pull the faces in across a few millimetres [S3, S4, read]. ModQuad uses a funnel-shaped "approach tunnel" and lets the magnets fix small misalignment [S6, read]. Most mobile modules in the docking literature assume they can drive anywhere (holonomic or differential-drive motion) [S3, read]; our bot cannot.
3. **Moving while docked is hard.** The successful systems (M-Blocks, ElectroVoxel, ATRON, Roombots, the 2026 rolling-sphere "stress-sharing" repair paper) move by *pivoting about a docked, fixed neighbour*, with a lattice that fixes where everything may go [S1, S5, S7, S17, S19, read]. They all keep strong dock faces and put the motor inside the module. Our bot's mobility (roll along one line using a swept arm) is different: I found no paper on it.

**What the mathematics says about strength.** Strength of a structure made of rigid bodies joined at faces is a well-studied topic with exact, cheap tools:

- *Rigid or floppy?* Rigidity theory counts bonds against freedoms. For a flat network of pin-jointed points, Laman's theorem says n points need 2n-3 well-placed bonds (and no over-crowded sub-group) to be rigid [S30, read]. If the bonds also resist twisting (a rigid weld), a connected network is rigid in a plane by counting alone; the question becomes how *stiff*. In 3D there is no complete counting rule for point-and-bar frameworks [S30, read], but for rigid bodies joined by bars or hinges there is one (Tay-Whiteley; see [S31, read], [S32, partly snippet]).
- *Triangles matter.* A square grid of pins can shear flat (4 neighbours is exactly at the threshold; a triangular grid with 6 neighbours is rigid). FreeSN says triangles in the structure "significantly improve the structural stability" [S20, read]. This is the engineering version of Maxwell counting [S35, snippet].
- *A single number that says "rigid" and is differentiable.* Zelazo et al. define a **rigidity eigenvalue**: a formation is infinitesimally rigid exactly when it is positive, and robots can follow its gradient, even from range measurements alone, in a distributed way [S24, S25, read]. Williams et al. have decentralized pebble-game tests and a test for *redundant* rigidity (still rigid if one link fails) [S28, read].
- *Mechanical checks designed for robots exist.* Blinky-Block researchers predict overloaded connections with a distributed beam model solved by the modules themselves [S22, read]; Wu et al. give a linear-time quasi-static test that returns an "internal stability margin" and which connections break first [S21, read].

**What I did not find.** No paper that applies a rigidity matrix to a *swarm that moves and then docks*, no neural cellular automaton trained with a stiffness or rigidity loss, and no work on one-line (rolling-ball) motion. That is the open space for this project.

### The five most promising ideas (ranked)

1. **Measure strength first, on the contact graph, and let docking order build a rigid skeleton (R1, R3, R7, R8).** Cheapest, most decisive, and standard in the field. A bot that docks to *two* already-docked neighbours that are not in a line adds a point to a rigid structure and keeps it rigid (Henneberg move; checked by Laman counting, R7). This is a purely local rule that guarantees something global. Why first: it needs no new physics, only a graph and a stiffness matrix, and it tells us at once whether "returns to the shape" already implies "strong" (I suspect not; R1).
2. **Separate moving from holding: move undocked, dock last, melt only the damaged edge (R6, R16).** A rigidly docked cluster can only move if its members' roll lines agree, so strength and mobility trade off directly. Repair with the existing mobile NCA, then "freeze" by docking in a good order. Why: explains the earlier failed docked-pair tests and fits the learned results (clumps are repaired, cuts are not).
3. **Use the arms as struts and as hinges, not as legs (R4, R5, R15).** The tip-docked 3-bot ring (62.5 mm side, from `embodiment.md`) is a triangle: the smallest rigid unit that works even with pin-like connectors. The tip collar rolls about the pole axis, so a tip-to-tip dock is naturally a hinge on the rolling axis: two bots on a shared axle can roll differentially. The earlier docked-pair tests used a rigid weld and a ball joint, neither of which is that hinge, and from my reading of `dock_pair.py` the arm doing the roll may be the arm being docked (not run; R5).
4. **Give the controller a stress or weak-cut signal (R9, R10, R11).** Local features may predict which bonds are critical; a Fiedler-vector or load-sharing channel can tell a neck to thicken. A differentiable stiffness term in the NCA loss is untried in the literature I found.
5. **Get a common axis frame without a compass from reciprocal bearings (R13), then use it for docking compatibility (R14) and for rotating a whole shape (R12).** Two neighbours measuring each other's bearing in their own frames reveal their relative heading directly; consensus on the doubled angle gives a shared axis direction.

### Plain-words primer (terms used below)

- **Rigid / floppy / floppy mode.** A network is rigid if, apart from moving or rotating as a whole, it cannot change shape. A *floppy mode* is one way it can still wiggle. Count them: zero floppy modes = rigid.
- **Pin joint vs rigid bond.** A pin lets neighbours swing about the joint; a rigid bond also resists swinging and shear (it transmits a moment). Magnet-and-ball docks are closer to pins unless the toothed face and the magnet preload carry a moment (R3).
- **Bridge / critical bond.** A bond whose removal splits or loosens the structure. **Minimum cut** = fewest bonds whose removal disconnects it. **Redundancy** = bonds beyond the minimum.
- **Stiffness matrix K.** Gives forces from small displacements; its small eigenvalues are the soft ways the structure can deform. **Compliance** = displacement under a standard load (inverse of stiffness).

## Section 3. Hypotheses

Format: statement / basis / cheap test / supports / refutes / effort. **Bold** = bold hypothesis. "Particle test" = graph or particle abstraction; "MuJoCo test" = fewer than 10 bots on the real CAD using `bot.py`, `multibot.py`, `dock_pair.py`.

A harness note used by many tests. **Static load test (MuJoCo):** weld one bot to the world (or hold it with a mocap body), apply a force with `xfrc_applied` at a far bot, ramped from 0 to 0.2 N over 5 s (a bot weighs about 0.19 N: 19.3 g), wait for rest, read displacement; stiffness = force / displacement; report in bot weights per mm. Connectors are modelled with `weld` (rigid), `connect` (pin/ball joint), or a weld with softer `solref` (to mimic finite stiffness); none of these is the real Hirth plus EPM face, which `FINDINGS.md` says is not modelled.

### Strength metrics and the graph

**R1. "Returns to the shape" does not imply "strong".** *Statement:* final configurations of the trained swarm, even when fidelity is good, score poorly on rigidity and redundancy compared with a hex-packed copy of the same shape; the gap grows with damage severity. *Basis:* strength is a property of bonds, which the current loss never sees; rigidity theory [S30, S24]; thin necks failing [S39]. *Cheap test (particle):* take saved final positions of the donut, lizard and the cut/squash/clump runs; build the contact graph (centres closer than 1.15 body diameters; stated assumption); compute the section-4 metrics for (a) the repaired swarm, (b) the undamaged control, (c) a hex-packed reference with the same n in the same shape, (d) random-removal controls. *Supports:* repaired and control swarms have a rigid fraction or Q_rel under, say, 0.5 of the hex reference and a large critical-bond fraction. *Refutes:* all within 10% of hex (the current objective already gives strength). *Effort:* 3-6 hours (metrics code is the work; the later tests reuse it).

**R2. At a given bot count, triangular packing gives much more strength than the square grid that four equatorial ports (90 degrees) encourage.** *Statement:* with pin-like docks, a square 4-neighbour arrangement has a shear floppy mode; a triangular arrangement (6 neighbours) does not; diagonal bracing by arm tips converts a square patch to a rigid one with few extra bonds. *Basis:* Maxwell/Laman counting [S30, S35 snippet], FreeSN triangles [S20], embodiment ports [`embodiment.md`]. *Test (particle):* 6x6 square patch vs triangular patch vs square patch plus k diagonal braces; count floppy modes (pebble game) and compute Q (section 4). *Test (MuJoCo):* 2x3 balls linked by `connect` at the port positions, with and without 1-2 tip diagonals (arm tip to the diagonal neighbour); static load test. *Supports:* square patch: floppy modes at least 1 and deflection grows without bound or by more than 5x versus braced; braced patch within 2x of triangular. *Refutes:* the square patch holds as well as the braced one under pin joints (would mean MuJoCo's contacts or friction, not geometry, carry the load: re-examine). *Effort:* 1 day.

**R3. The connector's moment stiffness decides whether the swarm behaves like a pin-jointed truss or a welded frame, and there is a sharp threshold.** *Statement:* sweeping the ratio of rotational to normal bond stiffness shows a jump in Q by orders of magnitude; and the abstract metric ranks MuJoCo configurations the same way the MuJoCo load test does (this doubles as validation of the metric). *Basis:* ATRON had large sag from connector play [S5]; EP-Face moment capacity collapses with misalignment and is weaker in shear [S4]; ball-and-magnet docks behave as spherical joints [S20]. My arithmetic: a 3 N EPM (the assumed value in `embodiment.md`) acting at a 5-8 mm face radius gives roughly 15-24 mN*m of holding moment; one bot hanging off a face at 30 mm lever loads it with 0.19 N x 0.03 m = 5.7 mN*m, and a three-bot cantilever with about 34 mN*m, so chains of three should fail without bracing. *Test:* (a) particle: sweep k_rot/k_n over 1e-3..1e2 on 3 shapes. (b) MuJoCo: 3-bot chain and 3-bot triangle, weld vs `connect` vs soft weld; load test; compare to (a) by Spearman rank over 20 random 4-5 bot graphs. *Supports:* threshold visible; rank correlation 0.8 or higher. *Refutes:* correlation under 0.5 (metric is not predicting physics; fall back to MuJoCo as the only judge). *Effort:* 1-2 days. **Outcome also gives the hardware requirement for EPM force.**

**R4. Arms as struts: a tip-docked 3-bot ring is the smallest rigid cell even with pin-like connectors.** *Basis:* triangle = 3 bots, 3 bonds = 2n-3, rigid by Laman [S30]; FreeSN strut-node triangles [S20]; the 62.5 mm triangle spacing in `embodiment.md`. *MuJoCo test:* 3 bots at the corners of the tip-docked triangle, `connect` constraints at the three tip pairs; load test vs a 3-bot tip-docked chain. *Supports:* triangle deflection under 20% of the chain's and no tumble. *Refutes:* the triangle sags or twists as much as the chain (would mean the arm joints, not geometry, are the limit; see R15). *Effort:* 3-5 hours. The abstraction test comes free with R2.

### Docking, turning and arm use

**R5. The failed docked-pair turn may have been a test-setup problem: dock on the roll axis through a free hinge, with the docking arm held still.** *Statement:* two bots docked tip to tip on a common pole axis behave like two wheels on one axle; if the dock allows relative rotation about that axis (the tip collar does exactly that), rolling them in opposite directions yaws the pair by about atan(2x15/82), i.e. about 20 degrees, using the far-side arms. *Basis:* `FINDINGS.md`: same-direction welded rolls moved the pair 28 mm with yaw -1.5 (consistent with a rigid axle); opposite rolls with a rigid weld tumbled it, and with a ball joint at the tips gave yaw -1 deg and 5 mm. From reading `dock_pair.py`: both bots run the identical script (top arm pitched 30 degrees, then azimuth +/-90), so in the end-to-end layout one bot's docked arm is probably also its rolling arm, which pitches its tip off the dock point; I did not run it to confirm. SMORES turns by differential drive of two wheels [S3, read], the same trick. *MuJoCo test:* in `dock_pair.py`, replace `weld`/`connect` by a hinge about the pole axis (an `<equality><joint>` or a `connect` plus a joint-limits-free hinge body), set the docked arms' pitch to 0, give the far-side arms the +/-90 azimuth sweep; run 5 repeats with friction 0.4-1.0. *Supports:* pair yaw 10-25 degrees, centre moves under 8 mm, both bots stay upright in 4 of 5 runs. *Refutes:* tumbling persists even with the docked arms idle. *Effort:* 3-6 hours. *Bold:* mildly (it reverses an earlier negative result).

**R6. Mobility and rigidity trade off: a rigidly docked cluster of one-line bots moves only if all their roll lines agree, so the swarm should move undocked, dock last, and melt only the damaged edge.** *Statement:* each bot may only translate perpendicular to its pole axis. A rigid cluster is a single rigid body, so its allowed translations are the intersection of its members' lines: a parallel-pole cluster can slide along the common perpendicular (the axle case), a cluster with two different pole directions has none. *Basis:* reasoning from the measured one-line motion [`FINDINGS.md`]; M-Blocks and the repair paper move by detaching and pivoting about fixed neighbours rather than by moving whole clusters [S1, S19]. *MuJoCo test:* two bots welded side by side with orthogonal pole axes; each runs its one-roll script; measure centre motion (expect near zero), then parallel poles (expect motion along the shared perpendicular). *Particle test:* add a "docked" flag with unlimited bonds; measure what fraction of random-heading rigid clusters can translate at all. *Supports:* orthogonal welded pair moves under 5 mm; random clusters of 3 or more members almost never movable. *Refutes:* the orthogonal pair moves (yaw freedom of a ball on a floor could evade the argument). *Effort:* 2-4 hours. **Consequence if supported:** strength requires a freeze phase, and repair requires thawing, so the NCA needs a "docked/undocked" output.

**R7. (Bold) A local docking rule "dock to two non-collinear already-docked neighbours" grows a rigid structure without any global check.** *Basis:* Henneberg vertex addition (new point, two bonds) preserves minimal rigidity in 2D [S30, the Laman characterization via constructions is standard; the specific move is from rigidity background, not read here]. With rigid bonds, one bond already suffices for rigidity but not for stiffness or redundancy, so use two. Geometry catch: ports are 90 degrees apart, so a body-port pair cannot form an equilateral triangle; the rule needs the arm-tip bonds (62.5 mm triangle) or a 45-degree-compatible layout. *Particle test:* seed triangle; bots arrive in random order within a docking radius; each docks per the rule, per a "dock to nearest" rule, and per "dock all within contact"; ports limited to 4 per bot (6 with arm tips); compare Q, critical-bond fraction, and bonds used. *Supports:* the rule gives full rigid fraction with about 2n bonds, "dock to nearest" leaves floppy modes or bridges. *Refutes:* ordering noise (random arrival) leaves the rule starved (few bots ever find two collinear-free partners), so rigid fraction under 70%. *Effort:* 1 day.

**R8. Greedy docking by marginal rigidity gain reaches the rigid state with fewer bonds than docking everything touching.** *Basis:* the pebble game tells whether a new bond is independent of the ones already accepted (about O(n^2) for a whole graph, per a search summary, not read; [S30] names the game but gives no bound); decentralized versions exist [S29 snippet, S28 read]. *Particle test:* given random touching graphs, compare (a) all bonds, (b) bonds accepted by a pebble-game independence check in random order, (c) (b) plus redundant bonds added until the redundant-rigidity test passes [S28]. Count bonds and EPM switch events (energy proxy). *Supports:* (b) reaches the same rigid fraction with 30-50% fewer bonds, (c) costs 1.2-1.4x (b). *Refutes:* no difference (dense packing is already redundant). *Effort:* 1 day.

**R9. Cheap local features predict which bonds are critical.** *Statement:* a bond's two-hop triangle count and local degree predict, with high accuracy, whether removing it creates a floppy mode or disconnects the structure; so an NCA can learn "I am a weak spot" from neighbour data alone. *Basis:* no source; reasoning from rigidity counting; contrast with the global tools in [S28, S29]. *Particle test:* on 2,000 random contact graphs from the existing simulator, label each bond critical/non-critical (rank drop or bridge); fit logistic regression on 1-hop and 2-hop features; report AUC. *Supports:* AUC at or above 0.9. *Refutes:* AUC under 0.75 (local info is not enough, so the controller must use a diffused signal as in R10). *Effort:* 3-5 hours.

**R10. (Bold) A diffusing "stress" or Fiedler-vector channel lets the swarm find and thicken a weak neck, which the shape-only loss cannot.** *Basis:* the repair paper moves modules toward damage by distress tokens [S19]; Fiedler value upper-bounds worst-case robustness but is not monotone in added edges [S36]; Smart Bricks failed at a narrow neck [S39]; distributed load solvers exist [S22]. *Particle test:* dumbbell shape, two lobes and a neck of width 1, 2, 3 bots; (a) baseline NCA behaviour, (b) a hand-written rule: each bot estimates the local Fiedler component by 20 consensus iterations on the contact-graph Laplacian and bots on the heavy side near the minimum cut move toward the cut when they have a free roll direction; compare Q and deflection. *Supports:* the neck gains at least 1 bot of width and Q_rel rises by 1.5x or more, with the lobes' fidelity loss under 5%. *Refutes:* conservation of bots means thickening the neck just thins the lobes; Q gain under 10%. *Effort:* 2-3 days.

### Learning, orientation and reshaping

**R11. A differentiable worst-mode-stiffness term in the training loss raises strength at a small cost in fidelity.** *Basis:* the rigidity eigenvalue is differentiable in positions and has a distributed gradient [S24, S25]; compliance-based losses work in topology optimisation [S50, snippet]; graph NCAs exist [S40, S41]; I found no NCA with this term. *Particle test:* in the existing trainer, add `-log(lambda_4(K_soft))` on the final positions, where bond weights are `w_ij = sigmoid((1.15D - d_ij)/tau)` (smooth contact), `K = sum w_ij * K_ij` (section 4), computed with `torch.linalg.eigvalsh`; weight 0.01-0.1; compare shape fidelity, repair gains, Q on 3 shapes. Start with n at most 150 for the matrix. *Supports:* Q_rel up by 30% or more, fidelity change within the seed spread (+/-0.025 in score, `FINDINGS.md` section 11). *Refutes:* eigenvalue gradient dominated by noise, training unstable, or the swarm packs to hex but loses the shape. *Effort:* 2-3 days.

**R12. (Bold) A compass-free swarm can rotate a whole shape by projecting a tangential command onto each bot's roll line.** *Basis:* rotation needs tangential motion; a one-line bot can move tangentially only if its pole is radial, and otherwise gives a zigzag with a radial part; alignment alone rarely produces rotation without relative position sensing (review summary, snippet [S48]); the swarm measures neighbour bearings anyway [S11]. *Particle test:* disc of 300 bots with random headings; each bot gets a target angular velocity field w x r (pure-rotation target as extra input; no training: first a hand rule moving along the line to reduce the tangential error, including a no-undocked-bonds constraint); measure rotation angle after 100 steps and shape drift. *Supports:* 30+ degrees of net rotation with area/shape change under 10%. *Refutes:* shape erodes (the line constraint makes pure rotation impossible without radial leakage). *Effort:* 1-2 days for the hand rule, 4+ for training.

**R13. Neighbours can recover each other's relative pole-axis heading from their two reciprocal bearings, and doubling the angle (because the bot is end-to-end symmetric) gives a common axis frame by simple consensus.** *Basis:* bearing-only SE(2) rigidity needs no common reference [S26, S27]; a homogeneous swarm can estimate global poses from local range and bearing, with comm loss [S11]; heading consensus on 10 robots [S48 snippet]; relative orientation from range [S12]. *Particle test:* 200 bots, random fixed pole angles, bearing noise 5 degrees, range noise 2 mm, 30% packet loss, 7 neighbours; run consensus on `exp(i*2*theta)`; report error over iterations and effect of noise. *Supports:* median axis error under 10 degrees within about 30 rounds. *Refutes:* if error plateaus above 20 degrees (then the frame must be built from structure, e.g. from docked neighbours). *Effort:* 3-6 hours. Note that bots cannot *rotate* to the frame cheaply (the turn gait costs about 4 s and 32 mm), so the frame is for choosing partners and commands (R6, R12, R14), not for aligning headings.

**R14. Docking success is set by the approach staging, and with stride noise a two-stage "coarse roll then capture" approach beats a single-stage one.** *Basis:* SMORES navigate/adjust/approach [S3]; ModQuad funnel [S6]; EP-Face capture over 4 mm normal and 7 mm sideways [S4]; Hirth teeth are self-centering [S47]; the bot's roll stride is 0.3 mm per degree with drift -0.0064*s^2 mm along the pole axis, but chained manoeuvres are unreliable [`FINDINGS.md`]. *Particle test:* two bots, one fixed, one rolling in strides with +/-20% length noise, 5 degree heading noise, drift as measured; capture if within r_c; plot success vs r_c (2-10 mm) for a one-stage and a two-stage (coarse then fine strides below 5 mm) policy. *MuJoCo test:* repeat with real roll strides for 10 trials. *Supports:* success of at least 90% with r_c near 4 mm in two-stage and under 50% in one-stage. *Refutes:* the pole-axis drift (no robust motion along the pole axis) makes capture impossible whatever r_c. *Effort:* 1-2 days.

**R15. Arm servos cannot carry structural load: strength has to come from the connectors and from locked-geometry struts.** *Basis:* my arithmetic from `bot.py`: servo torque 15 mN*m over the 22 mm arm (pitch) is about 0.7 N at the tip, about 3.5 bot weights, and that is the stall figure for a position servo with no gearbox backdrive data in the files; the cantilever numbers in R3 show chains need more than that. Chain-type modular systems report the same problem [S5]. *MuJoCo test:* hold a tip at a fixed point (connect to the world), load the body with 0.05-0.3 N, read the arm pitch joint error. *Supports:* deflection large at 0.2 N; therefore only locked or self-locking arms count as struts. *Refutes:* position servo holds within 1 mm at 0.3 N (then arms can actively brace). *Effort:* 2 hours. **Hardware question it exposes:** whether the real gearmotors are back-drivable.

**R16. Redundant rigidity predicts survival when 5-15% of bots fail; plain degree does not.** *Basis:* redundantly rigid networks remain rigid after one link loss [S28]; 5-15% failed modules was tolerated except at a neck [S39]; Fiedler value is not monotone in added edges [S36]. *Particle test:* on 500 random contact graphs, delete 5, 10, 15% of bots randomly; record the rigid fraction afterwards; compare predictors: mean degree, minimum cut, Fiedler value, critical-bond fraction, redundant-rigidity flag. Report Spearman rank. *Supports:* critical-bond fraction or the redundancy flag has rank correlation 0.2 or more above degree. *Refutes:* all predictors tie (use the cheapest). *Effort:* 4-6 hours.

**R17. Preload geometry: Hirth teeth plus EPM preload lock shear, so a docked bond transmits a moment only while preload exceeds the twisting load.** *Statement:* a docked face is rigid until a load threshold, then slips or opens; strength is therefore a threshold (load until first bond fails), not a stiffness. *Basis:* EP-Face holds moment only while the pole pieces overlap; its capacity drops to about 10% at 25 degrees [S4]; M-Blocks and ElectroVoxel note impulses exceed static forces [S1]; Hirth teeth are self-centering and backlash-free when clamped [S47]. *Test (particle):* extend the stiffness model with a per-bond break rule (normal force over F_max, or moment over M_max) and run a quasi-static load ramp that removes the most loaded bond each step [S21 gives a convex version]; report the load at first failure and at collapse; compare triangular and square layouts. *Supports:* collapse load differs 3x or more between layouts and between connector settings. *Refutes:* first failure equals collapse in all layouts (no redundancy benefit). *Effort:* 1-2 days.

## Section 4. Proposed geometric strength metric

### Required data
For one snapshot of the swarm (in the particle simulator or from MuJoCo):

- `pos`: (n, 2) bot centres in mm, floor plane (3D later).
- `bonds`: list of pairs (i, j) that are docked. In the particle simulator, where docking does not exist yet, use the proxy "centres closer than 1.15 body diameters (34.5 mm)". In MuJoCo use inter-bot contacts or the constraint list (`data.contact` between bot geoms; `data.efc_*` for equality constraints).
- Optional for R7, R8: `port_angle[i]` (the bot's pole heading, from the existing per-bot heading) to keep only bonds near the 4 port directions.
- Connector parameters `k_n, k_t, k_r` (normal stiffness N/mm, shear N/mm, rotational N*mm/rad). Defaults: k_t = 0.4 k_n (EP-Face shear/normal ratio [S4]), k_r = k_n * (face radius)^2 / 4 (my approximation; calibrate by R3).
- Optional: `target_pts` (target shape sampled) to build the reference lattice.

### Primary metric: relative worst-mode stiffness, Q_rel
Idea in words: build the springs-and-beams model of the docked graph, find its *softest* way to deform (excluding sliding or rotating as a whole), and divide by the same number for an ideal fully-docked packing of the same shape. 1 means as stiff as it could be, 0 means it has a floppy mode or is split.

```
def stiffness_matrix(pos, bonds, kn, kt, kr, L=29.0):
    n = len(pos); K = zeros(3n, 3n)             # DOFs per bot: u, v, phi
    for (i, j) in bonds:
        d = pos[j]-pos[i]; Lij = norm(d); e = d/Lij; t = [-e[1], e[0]]
        # relative motion at bond midpoint, in (normal, tangent, rotation)
        B = zeros(3, 6)
        B[0, [0,1]] = -e;  B[0, [3,4]] =  e              # normal stretch
        B[1, [0,1]] = -t;  B[1, [3,4]] =  t              # tangential slip
        B[1, 2] = -Lij/2;  B[1, 5] = -Lij/2              # ... including body rotations
        B[2, 2] = -1;      B[2, 5] =  1                  # twist
        Ke = B.T @ diag([kn, kt, kr]) @ B
        add Ke into K at DOF indices of i and j
    return K

def Q(pos, bonds, ref_pos, ref_bonds):
    lam   = eigvalsh(K(pos, bonds))              # sorted ascending
    lamR  = eigvalsh(K(ref_pos, ref_bonds))
    return lam[3] / lamR[3]                      # index 3: skip 3 rigid-body modes
```

Notes. (i) For 2 DOF per bot with only `kn` bonds, K reduces to R^T R (R the rigidity matrix), and `lam[3]` is the Zelazo rigidity eigenvalue [S24] (positive iff infinitesimally rigid). (ii) A disconnected graph has extra zero eigenvalues, so Q = 0 automatically; use the largest component for a size-weighted score if wanted. (iii) Small n (under 1,000): dense `eigvalsh` is fine; for larger, `scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh(K, k=5, sigma=-1e-6)`. (iv) Differentiable via `torch.linalg.eigvalsh` for R11 when bond weights are smooth. (v) Ideal reference: sample n points evenly in the target shape (hex lattice clipped to the shape), same 1.15 D bond rule. (vi) Q_rel is shape-aware on purpose: a long thin shape cannot be strong, and the metric says so in the reference.

### Secondary metrics

1. **Rigid fraction and floppy-mode count (exact, combinatorial).** Run the (2,3) pebble game on the bond graph (pin model) and the (3,3) game (rigid-bond model, as used for friction contacts in [S33]); output the size of the largest rigid cluster / n and the number of floppy modes `3n - 3 - rank`. Fast and independent of the stiffness numbers; data: `bonds` only (plus `pos` for the generic position caveat).
```
rigid_fraction = max_cluster_size(pebble_game(bonds, k=2, l=3)) / n
floppy = 2*n - 3 - number_of_independent_bonds        # pin model
```
2. **Redundancy / critical-bond fraction.** For each bond, test whether its removal increases floppy modes (pebble game: a bond is *redundant* if the (2,3) game rejects it in some order; a bond is *critical* if removing it lowers the independent count) or disconnects the graph (bridge via DFS low-link). Report `critical = #critical / #bonds` and `edge_connectivity = min cut` (Stoer-Wagner or a max-flow over a few candidate cuts). Data: `bonds`. Complexity about O(n^2) with a naive recheck; fine for under 500 bots. Interpretation: 0 critical bonds means the structure survives any one failed docking [S28].
3. **Standard-load compliance C (physical units).** Fix the 3 DOFs of the bot nearest the centroid, apply one unit load to the farthest bot (two perpendicular directions and a torque) and a uniform "wind" load on all bots (two directions); solve `u = K_reduced^{-1} f`; report `C = max over load cases of f^T u` normalised by `F^2 * L/k_n`, and the largest displacement in mm per 0.19 N (one bot weight). This is the number to compare with the MuJoCo static load test (R3); a stiff structure under test conditions gives small numbers, a floppy one gives infinity (use the pseudo-inverse and flag it).

Optional fourth if cheap: **Fiedler value** (second smallest eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian) to locate the weakest cut and to give the R10 channel; remember it is not monotone in added edges [S36].

### Where each metric runs
- Training: Q (smooth weights) as a loss or a validation score; secondary 1-2 as evaluation only (non-differentiable).
- Evaluation: all of them, plus the MuJoCo static load test as ground truth.
- MuJoCo: the metric reads the contact or constraint list, so the same code runs on both.

## Section 5. Two-week experiment plan

Ordering rule: cheapest and most decisive first; each step has a stop rule so we do not build on a wrong premise.

**Days 1-2: metrics and audit (no new physics).**
1. Implement `strength.py` with Q, rigid fraction, critical-bond fraction, compliance (section 4). Unit-test on a triangle (rigid), a chain (floppy), a square patch (pin: floppy; rigid bonds: stiff), a hex patch.
2. Run **R1** on existing final configurations (donut, lizard, cut/squash/clump runs). Decision gate: if repaired swarms already match hex within 10%, strength is a non-problem at this contact definition and priority shifts to R12/R13; otherwise continue.
3. Run **R9** and **R16** on random graphs (same code; hours).

**Days 3-5: the MuJoCo static load harness, then the cheap bodies.**
4. Build the static load test (section 3 harness) on `multibot.py`. Run **R15** (2 h) first: it fixes how much the arms can do.
5. **R3** (chain vs triangle, weld/connect/soft) to validate the metric by rank correlation; **R4** (tip-docked triangle); **R2** (square vs braced vs triangular). Decision gate: if rank correlation is under 0.5, stop using the abstraction and judge by MuJoCo.

**Days 6-7: revisit the failed docked pair.**
6. **R6** (orthogonal and parallel welded pairs) and **R5** (hinge on the roll axis with passive docked arms). Report five repeats each. If R5 works, record a clip, and update `FINDINGS.md` and the Robot tab; if R6 is supported, add a "docked/undocked" output to the planned cell design.

**Days 8-10: docking rules and orientation (particle abstraction).**
7. **R7** and **R8** (Henneberg docking, greedy rank-gain docking) with a 4-port budget plus arm-tip bonds.
8. **R13** (axis consensus under noise and loss) and **R14** (two-stage approach with measured stride noise and drift).

**Days 11-14: controller-level tests (only those that survived the gates).**
9. **R10** (Fiedler/stress channel and neck thickening) with the hand rule first, no training.
10. **R11** (differentiable stiffness loss) in the existing trainer at n at most 150, one shape, two seeds.
11. If time remains: **R12** (shape rotation by tangential projection) and **R17** (failure-ramp collapse load).

**What to write down at the end of week 2.** A one-page table: for each hypothesis, supported / refuted / untested, with the number, the number of repeats and seeds, and the caveat "single run" where it applies. Everything in MuJoCo is simulation on convex-hull meshes with a flat-disc toothed face and no EPM; hardware numbers (connector moment, servo backdrivability, floor friction) remain the main uncertainty and R3 and R15 should be turned into hardware measurement requests as soon as they are done.
